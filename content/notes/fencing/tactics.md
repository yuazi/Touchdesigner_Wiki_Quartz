Épée tactics are an ongoing conversation about distance, tempo, and risk. Without right of way, every action has to actually land first, which means the game collapses into a much simpler question: what can I do that lands before they can hurt me? The answer is almost always "attack the wrist, or wait for them to expose it."

---

## (y-) Foundational Ideas

### Distance (Mesura)

Three useful distances:

- **Long distance**: out of reach of a lunge. Safe but also useless. Most of the bout happens here while you set things up.
- **Lunge distance**: their wrist is reachable with a full lunge, their body with a balestra-lunge. The decision zone.
- **Close distance**: within a half-lunge or even an extension. Either someone is about to score or someone has badly misjudged.

The whole footwork game is about controlling which distance you are in at any given moment. Force them into lunge distance when you want to attack, hold them at long distance when you want to recover.

### Tempo

Tempo is the unit of time it takes for a single fencing action. A simple attack is one tempo. A parry-riposte is two tempos. If you can do something in fewer tempos than your opponent's response requires, you win the touch.

Most stop-hits in épée work because the stop-hit is one tempo (extend) and the opponent's attack is two (advance then extend). You hit them in the gap.

### First and Second Intention

A **first intention** attack is the one you want to land. You commit, you go for it.

A **second intention** action is a trap. You make a feint or a half-attack that you do not actually expect to score, in order to provoke their response. Their response opens the door for the real action.

Most high-level épée is second intention. You probe the wrist, they parry-riposte, you counter their riposte. The "attack" was the bait.

## (y-) Épée-Specific Patterns

### Attack on the Prep

They prepare their attack (advance, blade press, a small balestra). You attack into the preparation, before they have committed. Works because their hand is dropping or their distance is changing, and they cannot defend cleanly mid-step.

### Stop-Hit on the Wrist

The most common touch in épée. They extend toward your body. You extend toward their wrist. Both lines are valid; yours is shorter. Your point lands first.

### Point-in-Line as a Wall

Establish point-in-line and then retreat. They have to choose between running onto your point or doing something blade-active first. Both options cost them tempo and you have already won the distance game.

### Drawing a Committed Lunge

Bait them into a full-commit lunge by retreating just enough to stay out of distance. Their lunge ends short; their recovery is slow. You counter-attack into the recovery.

### Toe Touch

Drop the point onto their front foot. Hard to set up, but if their guard is high and their feet are still, the toe is wide open. Scores the same as a chest touch.

### Closing Seconds Doubling

Tied bout, last 10 seconds. Whoever attacks risks a double touch. If you are leading, a double does not change the standings (some formats) or actively helps you (others). If you are tied, a double sends the bout to priority. Knowing the scoring math is part of the tactics.

## (y-) Reading the Opponent

A few patterns worth watching for in the first 30 seconds of a bout:

- **What is their default parry?** Lateral or circular? Drilling their preferred parry tells you which compound attack to throw.
- **Where do they hold their blade?** High and forward suggests they want to attack with the point. Low and dropped suggests they want to bait stop-hits and parry-riposte.
- **Do they retreat to attack, or attack to attack?** Some fencers fall into their attack from the retreat (drawing you in then exploding forward). Others march straight at you.
- **What is their reset distance?** Some fencers can fight effectively from close distance. Others panic and fleche the moment you crowd them.

## (y-) Modern Style Notes

| Style            | What It Looks Like                                                                                                                |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cannone (FRA)    | Deep low en garde, patient blade work in the opening, then a single explosive coiled attack. Wins on physicality.                 |
| Borel (FRA)      | Tall, calm, dominates the wrist with prises de fer and small blade taps. Wears the opponent down rather than scoring big touches. |
| Santarelli (ITA) | Wrist sniper. Point-in-line specialist. Will counter-attack into 80 percent of attacks rather than parry.                         |
| Kano (JPN)       | Compact, fast hand, frequent toe touches. Wins on precision and unpredictability.                                                 |
| Bida (RUS)       | Long lines, passata-sotto when crowded, blade pressure to set up fleches.                                                         |
| Heinzer (SUI)    | Defensive baseline, counter-attack heavy, classical parry-riposte when forced.                                                    |

## (y-) Core Principles

- **Distance is everything.** Most touches are won or lost in the inch between "in range" and "out of range."
- **Tempo over speed.** A slow, well-timed action beats a fast, badly-timed one every time.
- **Patience scores.** The fencer who waits for the right moment beats the fencer who creates moments.
- **Blade tactility.** Feel their pressure through the blade. Most of what they are going to do, you can feel before you see it.
- **Never throw away your point.** If you parry, riposte. If you attack, recover. The point should always be pointing at something useful.
