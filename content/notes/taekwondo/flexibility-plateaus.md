Everyone stalls. You gain fast for six weeks, then nothing moves for two months and you start wondering whether your hips are just built wrong. Usually they are not. This page is the diagnostic list for when [[notes/taekwondo/front-splits|(y-) the front split]] or [[notes/taekwondo/side-splits|(y-) the side split]] stops moving.

---

## (y-) First, Check It Is Actually a Plateau

Range fluctuates day to day by a surprising amount. Sleep, hydration, training load, stress, and even time of day all move it. You are measurably more flexible in the evening than first thing in the morning.

So before diagnosing anything: **measure once a week, at the same time of day, after the same warm-up.** Three bad sessions in a row is noise. Six weeks of a flat weekly measurement is a plateau.

Measure something objective. Inches from the floor at the hip in a supported split, or a mark on the wall for kick height. "It feels tight" is not data.

---

## (y-) The Diagnostic List

Work down these in order. The common causes are near the top.

### 1. You are not recovering

The most frequent cause by far. Deep PNF work is training, and training needs recovery. If you are maxing out daily, the tissue never adapts and the nervous system stays defensive.

**Fix:** Alternate hard days and easy days. Two or three deep PNF sessions a week, light mobility on the others. Counterintuitively, most stalled people gain range by doing _less_ intense work more consistently.

### 2. It is passive-only

If you can sit in a wide straddle but cannot lift your leg anywhere near that height, you have built passive range with no active control over it. The nervous system will start refusing to give you more passive range it cannot control, because that is unsafe.

**Fix:** Add end-range strength. Straddle lift-offs, wall-release kick holds, Copenhagen planks, slow controlled leg raises. The active-passive gap closing is often what unlocks the next passive gain.

### 3. Your pelvis is in the wrong position

Anterior pelvic tilt (arched lower back) jams the femur into the front rim of the hip socket in every split position. Most people never check this because they cannot see themselves.

**Fix:** Film yourself from the side. If the lower back is arched in the frog stretch or the couch stretch, tuck the tailbone under and re-test. The instant gain is often several inches.

### 4. You are stretching the wrong thing

- Front split stuck with the back knee bent: it is your **hip flexors**, not your hamstrings.
- Side split stuck with a pinch at the front of the hip: it is **rotation and impingement**, not adductor length.
- Sharp electric sensation behind the knee: it is **nerve tension**, not muscle. Floss it, do not pull it.

**Fix:** Match the drill to the actual limiter. Filming yourself and looking at where the position fails is the fastest way to identify it.

### 5. Your core cannot hold the position

Research on high-level martial artists keeps finding that core stability training improves kick height more than additional pure flexibility work. Your brain will not release hip range while the trunk feels unstable there.

**Fix:** Planks, deadbugs, bird dogs, and hollow holds, paired directly with the stretching session rather than on a separate day.

### 6. You changed nothing for three months

Tissue adapts to the stimulus it is given, then stops responding. The same five stretches at the same intensity for a year is not a program.

**Fix:** Change one variable. Swap passive holds for PNF, add load with Cossack squats, add elevation with blocks, change the angle of the foot, or change the tempo. Novelty in the stimulus restarts adaptation.

### 7. You are training through a low-grade strain

A muscle that is slightly torn will protectively tighten, and stretching it harder keeps re-injuring it. The tell is a stretch that feels sharp and localised at one spot, usually near the attachment point, and does not warm up like normal tightness does.

**Fix:** Two weeks of no deep stretching on that muscle, light isometrics only, then rebuild. This is the one case where more stretching is definitively the wrong answer. Persistent sharp pain at an attachment point is worth a physio's opinion rather than a note page's.

## (y-) The Ceiling That Is Real

Some limits do not move:

- **Bone geometry.** Femoral neck angle and hip socket orientation are individual and fixed. Some people cannot achieve a flat neutral side split at any amount of training.
- **Age of onset.** Starting at 30 means slower gains than starting at 12. Slower, not impossible.
- **Genetic collagen laxity.** Some people are simply built loose and some are built tight.

None of this stops you kicking to head height. A well-controlled 160 degree straddle with strong active range produces higher, faster, more usable kicks than a passive 180 with no strength behind it. Chase usable range, not floor contact.

---

## (y-) Recovery Levers That Actually Matter

| Lever          | Why It Moves Range                                                                        |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sleep          | Tissue repair and nervous-system downregulation both happen here. The single biggest one. |
| Hydration      | Dehydrated fascia is stiffer and less compliant.                                          |
| Protein intake | Connective tissue remodelling needs the raw material.                                     |
| Deload weeks   | Every 6 to 8 weeks, halve the intensity for a week. Range often jumps afterwards.         |
| Heat           | A warm shower or bath before evening stretching adds usable range immediately.            |

Foam rolling is worth a mention: it produces a real short-term increase in range with no loss of power, which makes it genuinely useful in a warm-up. It does not produce long-term length changes on its own. Use it as a door-opener before the stretch, not as the stretch.

## (y-) A Realistic Timeline

| Time      | What Progress Actually Looks Like                                    |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Week 1-2  | Range feels better within a session but resets by the next day       |
| Week 3-6  | First permanent gains. Usually fast. This is mostly nervous system.  |
| Month 2-4 | Slower, steadier gains. This is where tissue adaptation starts.      |
| Month 4-6 | Front split within reach for most people who trained consistently    |
| Month 6+  | Side split still grinding. This is normal and not a sign of failure. |

The fast early progress is nervous-system permission, and it runs out. What follows is slower because it is actual tissue remodelling, and that timescale is months, not weeks. Nearly everyone who quits does so at the handover between the two.

---

## (y-) The Short Version

- Measure weekly, not daily.
- Recover harder than you stretch.
- Build strength in the range you already have.
- Check your pelvis on film.
- Change one variable every couple of months.
- Sharp localised pain means stop, not push.
